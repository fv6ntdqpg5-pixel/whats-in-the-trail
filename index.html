What's in the Trail?

Why one aircraft draws a line across the whole sky and the next leaves nothing. What it would take to spray from airliners, counted in gallons, trucks, mines and people. And which programs really do put material in the air. Every number here can be changed or checked.

This is a slice of sky 190 miles long, seen from the side. The pale patches are pockets of air that hold more water vapour than ice can sit in balance with. Forecasters call them ice-supersaturated regions. They are clear air, invisible until an aircraft flies through one. Cold enough to start

Burning jet fuel makes water: about one gallon of water for each gallon of fuel. The exhaust leaves hot and wet, and as it mixes with the cold air behind the engine it can pass through fog conditions for a moment, the way breath does on a winter morning. The droplets freeze almost at once. That needs air colder than roughly −40 to −50°, and the two temperature scales meet at −40. The exact line shifts with pressure, humidity and engine efficiency. The rule was worked out by Schmidt in 1941 and Appleman in 1953.

Humid enough to stay

Once the ice crystals exist they either shrink or grow. Below 100% humidity against ice they turn back to vapour in seconds to a few minutes, and the trail is a short tail. Above 100% they pull water out of the surrounding air, grow and spread into a band. Trails tracked by satellite last 3.7 hours on average, and one was followed for 18. Airliners on North Atlantic routes were measured spending about 14% of cruise time inside such pockets, which average roughly 560 m (1,800 ft) deep. That is why a trail can stop and restart, and why two aircraft a few thousand feet apart can leave different trails.

A lasting trail is mostly water that was already there

This applies to the long trails that spread. The engine supplies the first ice crystals. In a humid pocket those crystals keep growing on vapour the air was already carrying. Aircraft that have flown through such trails with instruments found far more ice than the engines put out: more than a hundred times as much in the IPCC's 1999 aviation report, about ten thousand times in Knollenberg's 1972 paper. Some real secrets did last decades. Operation Sea-Spray stayed hidden for 26 years and the zinc cadmium sulfide tests for about 40. Each was a short run of tests by a small unit, and then it stopped. A spray program would have to run every day, at civil airports, with a workforce that keeps turning over.

Check it yourself

The first three show that how a trail looks, whether long, short or broken, is set by the air it was laid in. That makes the look of a trail no evidence of spraying either way. The last three are records anyone can read. Whether something is being added is the question the ledger answers.

Read the balloon data on a trail day

The National Weather Service normally launches balloons twice a day from about 90 sites and publishes every reading. The University of Wyoming's sounding archive lists them by station. Find the rows between 30,000 and 40,000 ft (300 to 200 hPa).

Predicted: on days with long spreading trails, a layer up there reads moist. Balloon humidity is given against liquid water, so ice saturation shows as about 68% at −40 °C, 62% at −50 °C and 58% at −60 °C. On short-trail days the whole band reads well below that. Balloon sensors tend to read dry in very cold air, so a reading a little under these values still counts.

Look up the aircraft

Open a flight tracking app when two aircraft are overhead, one with a long trail and one without. Tap each and note its altitude.

Predicted: they are at different heights, often 2,000 ft or more apart, or one is climbing or descending, or they are miles apart in different air. On a standard day no jet leaves a trail below roughly 30,000 ft. On a cold winter day trails form several thousand feet lower.

Watch a gap

Find a trail with a clean break in it. Watch the break for a few minutes, then watch the next aircraft on the same track.

Predicted: the gap drifts with the wind at that height along with the rest of the trail, and the next aircraft at that height shows a gap in the same patch of air. The gap belongs to the air.

Test rainwater the way a lab would

If you send a sample out, ask for silicon and iron as well as aluminium, barium and strontium. Use the lab's own bottle. In one survey 15 of 28 deposition scientists said a glass jar with a metal lid would contaminate the sample.

Predicted: in an unfiltered sample, aluminium far above barium, by tens to hundreds of times, with silicon and iron alongside. That is the mix in ordinary soil dust. Earth's crust is 8.2% aluminium, 425 ppm barium and 370 ppm strontium.

Read the weather modification filings

Since 1972 anyone who modifies weather in the United States must report it to NOAA, and NOAA posts the filings in its library. Search for your state.

Predicted: winter cloud seeding in western mountain states, hail and rain projects in Texas and North Dakota, each naming the sponsor, the target area and the grams of seeding agent. Nine states had active programs in 2024.

Find the old photographs

Search archives for bomber formations over Europe in 1943 and 1944, or read the September 1942 U.S. report on the problem, titled Condensation Trails: Where They Occur and What Can Be Done About Them.

Predicted: piston-engine bombers leaving long, spreading trails that cover the sky, more than fifty years before the word chemtrail. The first trail on record was seen in 1915.

Fair questions, with numbers

"I don't remember skies like this when I was a kid"

There are 3.5 times as many airline departures as in 1980: 10.7 million then, 37.4 million in 2024. Engines changed too. A more efficient engine puts less heat into its exhaust, so the exhaust reaches fog conditions in slightly warmer air. In a 1999 test a new Airbus A340 and a 1968 Boeing 707 flew side by side, and in one thin band of heights the Airbus left a trail while the Boeing left none. More flights at heights where trails form means more trails.

"They switch it on and off"

A trail that stops and restarts is the commonest piece of evidence offered, and it is what a patchy humid layer produces. Measured from aircraft, these pockets average about 150 km (93 miles) along the flight path, with many far smaller, and about 560 m deep. Aircraft cross their edges constantly.

"How do you know it's humidity and not a schedule?"

Because a humidity forecast predicts where trails will appear. In 2023 American Airlines pilots used such forecasts to climb or descend around the pockets on 22 flights. Satellite images showed 54% less trail per mile than on 22 matched flights that flew as normal. It was a small trial, and larger ones have followed. A forecast of humidity could not steer around a spray schedule.

"So trails do change the weather"

Yes, and that is published science. Lasting trails spread into thin cirrus that traps heat. Estimates of that warming run from somewhat less than to roughly double the effect of all the carbon dioxide aviation has ever emitted, and the uncertainty is wide. About two flights in a hundred cause 80% of it. Jet exhaust also carries soot and sulfur: fuel may hold up to 3,000 ppm sulfur and typically holds about 600.

"It's in the fuel, so there are no tanks to find"

Then the amounts are tiny. Jet fuel is made to a published specification and its approved additives are capped at milligrams per litre. At the 5 mg cap for the static additive, a 737 would put about half an ounce an hour through its engines, and every litre of jet fuel on Earth together would carry about 2,000 t a year. Fuel loaded with 1% of something extra would send 55 lb an hour of it through the filters, the fuel nozzles and the turbine. Engine soot does carry traces of metal from wear and oil, and that is measured and published.

"The Manhattan Project stayed secret"

For about three years, in wartime, with its own towns behind fences. It employed 125,000 people at its peak. Soviet agents were inside it by 1944. It ended with a public announcement, and some 500,000 people had worked on it. The secret tests in the record above stayed hidden for anywhere from 4 years to more than 50. They were small, they ended, and they still came out.

"Geoengineering is real, they admit it"

The research is real and its size is public. The deliberate stratospheric releases on record by mid-2025 add up to roughly 100 kg of sulfur dioxide, nearly all from one two-person company launching weather balloons. The engineering study of a real program calls for a new type of aircraft flying at 65,000 ft, far above airliners, and says it would be easily detectable.

"Why pass laws against it, then?"

Florida banned weather modification and geoengineering in July 2025 and opened a public reporting line. By March 2026 the state had received more than 20,000 complaints and said it had no report or evidence of anyone holding or operating the banned equipment. Florida had no active weather modification licences before the ban either.

"What would change your mind?"

The things every real program left behind: a purchase order, a tank certificate, a load sheet with unexplained weight, a mechanic's photo of plumbing that is not in the manual, or air samples taken inside a trail showing metals far beyond the traces that engine wear and soil dust account for. In a 2016 survey 76 of 77 atmospheric chemists and geochemists said they had seen no such evidence. The one who had cited unusually high barium in a single remote location.
